Music has never generated more money. The people who build the instruments have never captured less of it.

The popular framing — streaming boomed, physical died, gear died with it — is wrong in two of its three clauses.

The dynamic is not decline but value migration. Music's economics moved from ownership to access, and access accrues to rights-holders and platforms. Hardware sits at the least monetizable point in the chain: bought once, by a creator population growing in headcount while spending less per head.

A phone plus a subscription DAW plus one interface now clears a bar that once required a studio.

Two economic shocks sit underneath much of the distress of the last eighteen months.

Lockdowns plus stimulus payments produced the largest demand spike in the industry's modern history. The problem is the shape, not the size. A first-time-buyer surge inside a lockdown window is a cohort, not a run-rate. When the spike reverted, the industry did not face a normal downturn — it faced the absence of demand it had already served.

Every major sponsor entry in music software happened at or immediately after the peak. Those prices were underwritten against spike revenue, and the leverage placed on top of them assumed that revenue would persist. It did not, and debt service is indifferent to the reason.

The second shock was the tariffs. There was no real volume growth anywhere in the US channel.

So I built the document I wanted to read. Forty-three transactions in one dated record, each carrying a link to the filing or report it rests on. Thirty-one have a primary source; the other twelve do not, and the table says so in the same column rather than in a caveat at the end — because a reader deciding how much weight a number carries is looking at the number, not at the appendix.

If your board is planning against this market: when someone asks where a number came from, can you point at the filing?

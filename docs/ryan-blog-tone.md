# Ryan's writing tone — copywriting guide for `wp-website`

**Purpose.** A house style guide for any copy written in this repo, derived from Ry's own
published writing rather than from general "good marketing copy" advice. When a session
here drafts or edits prose — landing-page sections, hero copy, READMEs that a client might
read, a new blog post — this is the voice to match.

## 1. The voice in one paragraph

A senior practitioner explaining, without ceremony, something he did last week and what it
means for your business. He is unimpressed by the industry he works in and says so. He
names the wrong idea before he gives you the right one. He gives real numbers, hedged
honestly, and tells you the limits of the claim in the next sentence rather than burying
them. He never sells the future; he sells the smallest next step. The register is
**plain-spoken executive**, not academic and not hype.

---

## 2. Point of view

Four voices, each with a job. Mixing them up is the fastest way to sound wrong.

| Voice | When | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Narrating something Ry personally did or observed | "I recently ran an AI coaching session with the CEO of an 8-figure e-commerce company" · "In my own work, adopting AI-assisted coding increased my output by two to three times" |
| **We** | Wolfpack doing client work, or walking the reader through a build | "We work with leaders and teams to identify where AI can immediately reduce tedious work" · "we start with three primary databases" |
| **Wolfpack** (third person) | Stating the offer, usually near the close | "Wolfpack Data & Strategy helps SMBs adopt AI from the bottom up" |
| **You / your** | Sparingly, and mostly at the end | "If your organization is still using AI lightly or inconsistently…" |

**The reader is described in the third person for most of the piece** — "founders,
executives, and technology leaders," "leaders," "most SMBs," "small teams, founders,
operators, consultants" — and only becomes "you" as the piece turns toward the ask. Opening
a piece with "you" is off-voice.

**Name the reader by role, not by pain.** He writes "for many founders, executives, and
technology leaders," never "if you're a frustrated founder drowning in busywork."

---

## 3. Sentence mechanics — the hard rules

These are checkable. Run them like a lint pass.

1. **No em dashes or en dashes.** Zero across all three posts. Use a period, a comma, or a
   colon. This is the single most reliable tell of off-voice copy in this repo.
2. **No exclamation points.** Zero.
3. **No rhetorical questions.** Zero question marks in all three post bodies. He does not
   ask "So what does this mean for your business?" — he tells you.
4. **Contractions: effectively none.** He writes "does not," "is not," "was not," "do not."
   The single exception across ~1,950 words is the conversational opener "I'm 3 episodes
   deep." Default to the expanded form.
5. **Colons introduce; they do not dramatize.** "It should be a practical enablement process
   focused on improving the workflows employees already use every day." A colon sets up a
   list or an expansion: "three primary databases:" / "the skills transfer immediately
   across executive and operator workflows: diligence, finance, operations…"
6. **Quotation marks hold a phrase at arm's length.** Reserved for language he is rejecting
   or quoting from the industry: the word "integration," a generic "here's how to use AI"
   walkthrough, the goal was not just to "ask Claude questions." Never for emphasis.
7. **Short paragraphs — 1 to 4 sentences.** Single-sentence paragraphs are used as beats:
   "That is not the right starting point." · "That part matters."
8. **Active, declarative, subject-verb-object.** Subordinate clauses are rare.

## 4. Signature moves

Four patterns do most of the rhetorical work. Use them; do not use anything flashier.

### 4.1 The reversal — name the wrong frame, then the right one
His most-used device, in all three posts, usually with the same sentence opening repeated:

> "The goal was not just to 'ask Claude questions.' The goal was to teach a repeatable workflow"

> "The goal is not to use AI for its own sake. The goal is to make the business easier to operate."

> "The goal is not to make Notion cute or overly complicated. The goal is to build a practical command center"

> "This was not a generic 'here's how to use AI' walkthrough. This was practical executive AI enablement:"

> "The objective is not to force a large transformation before the organization is ready. The objective is to help the company understand AI through real work"

Two sentences, parallel openings, negative first. Do not soften it into "it's less about X
and more about Y" unless you are explicitly marking a paradigm shift — he saves that
construction for exactly that: "early AI adoption is less about connecting platforms and
more about connecting employees to better ways of working."

### 4.2 The fragment triple — for the anti-hype beat
> "Not hype. Not vague transformation theater. Just practical AI workflows applied directly to real business problems with real money, real deadlines, and real operational pressure."

Sentence fragments, in threes, ending on the affirmative. Use once per piece at most.

### 4.3 The concrete run-on list — proof by specificity
Long comma lists of real artifacts, inline in the prose rather than bulleted. They are the
argument: they show he knows the actual work.

> "delays, budget creep, platform conflicts, broken APIs, unclear ownership, and workflows that become more complicated before they become useful"

> "email, documents, codebases, meeting transcripts, spreadsheets, proposals, reports, customer notes, and content libraries"

> "diligence, finance, operations, vendor research, board prep, customer analysis, internal documentation, strategic planning"

Six to nine items. Concrete nouns only. No adjectives.

### 4.4 The immediate caveat
A claim is followed in the very next sentence by its limit. Never a footnote, never omitted.

> "This does not replace engineering judgment."

> "This does not remove the human element."

> "These materials still require review, and legal documents still require appropriate oversight, but AI can dramatically reduce the time…"

## 5. Claims, numbers, and evidence

- **Numbers are specific and honestly hedged.** "roughly $200,000" · "an 8-figure e-commerce
  company" · "In under 30 minutes" · "two to three times within the first couple of weeks" ·
  "five to ten times depending on the task" · "3 episodes deep." Ranges and approximators
  are a feature. Never round up into a cleaner-sounding number.
- **Attribute what you cannot stand behind yourself.** "In his words, the workflow could have
  saved him a month." He does not assert the month; the client did.
- **Clients are anonymized by shape, never named.** "the CEO of an 8-figure e-commerce
  company." Do not invent a logo, a name, or a testimonial attribution.
- **Value is stated in the reader's operational terms** — time saved, money on the table,
  output multiple, decision quality — never in feature terms.
- **Deflate the mystique explicitly.** "A lot of business AI value is not magic. It comes from
  knowing how to frame the problem, how to feed the model the right context, and how to make
  sure the company's existing knowledge actually gets used."
- **Name the actual tool.** "Claude," "Google Drive," "Notion." Not "an AI assistant," not
  "leading LLM platforms."

## 6. Vocabulary

**Load-bearing words** (recur across posts, and carry the positioning):
practical · workflow · real / actually · foundation · literacy · enablement · repeatable ·
usable · command center · friction / high-friction · tedious · operating leverage ·
bottom-up · internal champions · context

**Words the writing is built to reject.** Ry names these as the enemy, so never write them
straight: hype · magic · transformation theater · cute · seamless · revolutionary ·
game-changing · unlock · supercharge · cutting-edge · "leverage" as a verb · "empower" ·
"synergy" · "10x" as a slogan (he writes "five to ten times depending on the task" instead).

**Never open with throat-clearing.** No "In today's fast-paced business environment," no "AI
is changing everything," no dictionary definitions.

---

## 7. Structure of a piece

1. **Open cold, on something concrete.** A thing he did ("I recently ran an AI coaching
   session with…"), a status update ("I'm 3 episodes deep on…"), or a provocation with an
   immediate reversal ("the word 'integration' is a four-letter word. Good news, the
   'integration' is dead, and AI killed it.").
2. **Establish the wrong assumption, generously.** He never makes the reader feel stupid for
   holding it: "it is reasonable that many assume this means another large technical
   integration." The enemy is the vendor, the hype, and the complexity — never the reader.
3. **Body: subheads over bullets.** Section headings are Title Case plain statements or
   labels, never questions and never clever: *Start With Existing Workflows* · *Practical
   Examples* · *The First AI Investment Should Be Literacy* · *How Wolfpack Can Help*.
   Bulleted lists are rare — only the Notion post uses one (Products / Projects / Tasks),
   for three genuinely parallel nouns. Prefer a subhead or a comma list.
4. **Close on the smallest next step, not the vision.** "the best first step is not a major
   integration. It is a focused session around one employee, one workflow, and one task
   that can become faster, cleaner, and more creative."
5. **One CTA, plainly labeled.** "Work With Wolfpack" · "Contact Wolfpack". Three words,
   imperative, no urgency language, no scarcity, no "Book now." A short setup sentence
   precedes it: "If you are interested in learning AI by working on actual projects,
   Wolfpack can help."

**Length:** 350–1,200 words. Long enough to carry a real example, short enough to read in
two minutes.

## 8. Applying this to pages in this repo

Landing pages are not blog posts — they are shorter, scannable, and structurally
constrained. What carries over and what does not:

**Carries over unchanged:** the mechanics in §3 (no em dashes, no exclamation points, no
rhetorical questions, no contractions), the vocabulary in §6, the reversal in §4.1 for
section intros, the honest-number discipline in §5, and the plain three-word CTA label.

**Adapts:** page copy addresses "you" earlier and more often than a post does — a landing
page is already a direct address. Keep the role-naming from §2 in headers and hero copy.

**Does not apply — repo rules win:**

- **`hire/`** — experience bullets and project blurbs are **verbatim from the résumé YAML**
  and guarded by `ryan-resume-dev/resume_build/verify_facts.py`. Do not tone-edit them.
  Copy, do not paraphrase. Tone guidance applies only to the connective page copy around
  them.
- **`rates/`** — derived from `wolfpackdata/wp-rates-page` under
  [`docs/public-rates-consistency-contract.md`](public-rates-consistency-contract.md).
  *Depth may differ, facts may not.* This guide can shape how a fact reads, never which
  facts appear.
- **CTA policy** — the book-first, single-calendar-CTA rule in
  [`CLAUDE.md`](../CLAUDE.md) governs what a CTA points at. This guide governs only how it
  is worded.

## 9. Pre-ship checklist

- [ ] Zero em dashes and en dashes
- [ ] Zero exclamation points
- [ ] Zero rhetorical questions
- [ ] Contractions expanded
- [ ] Quotation marks only around language being held at arm's length
- [ ] At least one reversal (§4.1); at most one fragment triple (§4.2)
- [ ] Every number specific and hedged, not rounded up for effect
- [ ] Every strong claim followed immediately by its limit (§4.4)
- [ ] No client named; no testimonial invented
- [ ] No word from the reject list in §6
- [ ] Opens cold on something concrete; no throat-clearing
- [ ] Closes on the smallest next step, then exactly one plainly-labeled CTA
- [ ] Paragraphs 1–4 sentences; subheads are Title Case statements, not questions

Case Study · 2026 · Ryan Hickey

The Complaint Was a Specification

Project management is two jobs braided into one title: the record-keeping that fills most of the week, and the judgment that decides whether the project finishes. Wolfpack AI Command unbraids them — supervised AI operators run the record, inside written rules they are themselves governed by, so the half of the role that always needed a human gets its hours back.

01 · The consensus

Nobody argues about whether the role matters

Ask anyone who has run a project at any size what decided how it went, and they name a person. Not a methodology, not a tool, not a template. They name the person who knew what was blocked before anybody reported it, who told a client bad news early enough that it was still just news, and who carried the whole shape of the work while everyone else held their own piece of it.

The second thing they agree on is that the job is punishing. A project manager owns dates they cannot personally move, made of work they are not personally doing. The interruptions are the job, so there is no version of the week where the deep work happens first. And the output is invisible when it goes well: a project that lands on time looks, from outside, like a project that was always going to.

This is not a complaint about the people who take the role. It is an observation about a job that was assembled badly, and the people in it have been saying so for years. Burnout here is not a resilience problem. It is what happens when you braid two incompatible kinds of work together and hand them to one person with one calendar.

02 · The split

Two jobs braided into one title

Take a week in the role apart and it separates cleanly into two different jobs that happen to share a job title. They need different skills, they run on different clocks, and they fail in different ways.

  • The record

    Most of the hours

    • Chasing statuses nobody updated
    • Keeping tickets honest against what is actually true
    • Linking work to the plan it belongs to
    • Writing down what was decided, and where
    • Version bookkeeping and release notes
    • Remembering the thing that was ruled out in March
  • The judgment

    Almost none of them

    • Relationships, inside the team and outside it
    • Scheduling clairvoyance — seeing the slip three weeks early
    • Confident vertical communication, up and down
    • Enough technical breadth to know what is really being said
    • Deciding what gets cut, and defending it
    • Judging when a plan has stopped being true

The tedium is most of the job. It is none of the reason you hire a great project manager, and none of the reason anybody becomes one.

That is the complaint every good project manager I have worked with makes, usually about the fourth hour of a Friday spent reconciling a board against reality. It is worth taking seriously rather than sympathizing with, because it is precisely correct: the left-hand column is not what the role is for, it is what the role is made of.

And here is the trap. The left-hand column cannot simply be dropped, because the record is the substrate the judgment runs on. Scheduling clairvoyance is not a psychic gift — it is what pattern recognition looks like when it is fed an honest, current, complete account of what has been happening. Cut the record-keeping and you do not free the judgment. You blind it. So the record has to be kept, and kept well, by someone. It just never needed to be the same someone whose real value is the other column.

03 · What it costs

Under-resourced at every size, for the same reason

Excellence in the role demands both columns, and the left one looks like overhead on a budget line. So organizations at every scale resolve it the same way: they under-resource the role and hope.

A startup omits it outright — there is no project manager, there is a founder doing it badly at eleven at night. A small or mid-size business hands it to someone as a side-duty, at the ten percent of attention left over, which is exactly enough to keep a board looking plausible and nowhere near enough to keep it true. And the solopreneur developer is their own project manager by definition, for an audience of one, after the real work is finished.

What follows is attritional rather than dramatic, and it is the same at all three sizes. Two people build the same thing because nobody linked the tickets. A workstream stalls for three weeks because whoever would have noticed was in a different fire. A decision is made properly, in a thread, then evaporates and gets made again, differently, in April. And “done” comes to mean five different things depending on who is saying it.

In my own client history with startups, fewer than half the projects I have watched start have finished. That is my own book, not a study, and the sample is startups because that is the population I have watched closely enough to count. I would not extend the number to an SMB or to a solo developer. I would extend the pattern to both without hesitation, because the mechanism producing it has nothing to do with company size.

The honest limit of that number

What a completion rate does and does not tell you

Some of those projects deserved to die, and killing one early is a success that looks identical to a failure in a completion count. The figure is not an indictment of the founders involved and it is not a benchmark to plan against.

What it is good for is the shape of the thing. Almost none of the projects I watched stall did so because the work was too hard. They stalled because nobody was holding the whole picture.

04 · The bet

A command center, not an assistant

The obvious move in 2026 is to bolt an AI chat onto the chaos and ask it to help. I did not want that — not because it fails, but because it works well enough to be dangerous. It writes into your system of record with no identity, no rules it answers to, and no trail anyone can audit afterwards. You get a faster mess.

Most AI adoption starts from what can the AI do? That is the wrong first question for anything touching the record a business runs on. The questions a company leader actually has are older than AI:

  • What is it allowed to do? Not what it is capable of — what the rules permit, and where those rules are written down.
  • Who can tell what it did? Six weeks later, from the record itself, without asking anyone.
  • How would we know if it went wrong, and how hard is it to put back?

So the bet was to build the answers first and the automation second, binding three things together: the system of record — Notion, where projects, tasks, products and clients live; the system of work — GitHub, where the code and its history live; and the AI operators — Claude as the working agent, a different vendor's model as an independent reviewer, and plain Python where code is the right tool. What binds them is not a prompt. It is a body of written, versioned rules the AI is itself governed by.

The rules are not a system prompt somebody tuned. They are documents, in version control, reviewed and released like software — and the thing they govern is the AI.

The whole of it — the workspace governance, the code governance, the agents, and the skills that carry the rules into a live session — is Wolfpack AI Command. One source of truth, a thin pointer on every surface that needs to read it, and nothing that matters existing in two places.

05 · The system

Three layers, one rulebook

Three layers, none of them planned as a layer. Each exists because the one below it turned out not to be the right place to solve something.

The workspace layer — where the business is

Four databases carry the business: projects, tasks, products and clients. Every page is created from its database's template, never blank, because a blank page silently loses the information surface the template carries — a project page that shows its own tasks, a product page that shows both.

Nothing exists unlinked either: every task links both a project and a product, resolved by a ladder — stated, then deduced, then a placeholder created under standing permission, always announced, and marked with a prefix that exists to flag it for my review.

Every entry carries its database's icon, so an open browser tab is identifiable by kind at a glance, and the per-type distinction is pushed onto a governed color legend so the two signals never compete. The legend records my reasoning rather than asserting a convention:

  • Projects

    “Project completion exists in the akashic record, where truth meets experience.”

  • Tasks

    “Tasks tap into our creativity, bringing ideas to the material world.”

  • Clients

    “Neutral and fair — brown mixes all the colors of the rainbow.”

  • Products

    “Products interact with people, and the point is to forgive some kind of suffering and create customer success — green is the color of love & forgiveness.”

Those four sentences are mine, and a rule with its reasoning attached survives a new person, a new session and a new year.

All of it — identity, icons, template-first creation, interlinking, required properties, the status lifecycle, code linking, comment protocol, the review workflow — is published as one canonical rulebook: 54 numbered rules across ten lettered sections, read by every surface and compiled into eight skills that carry the relevant part into whichever session needs it. A read-only, offline, credential-free validator then checks that rulebook's duplicated copies against each other across five classes of drift. It was written after drift was found, not speculatively, which is the only reason I trust it.

The code layer — where the work is

The second layer's first rule is a scar. An AI session working in one repository with a subfolder selected inferred the repository name from the folder name — the most salient string in its context — and filed a well-formed, correctly-labeled issue against a repository that does not exist. Nothing was broken. Nothing was noticed for a while either, which is the more interesting half.

Resolve the repository from git; never guess became the founding rule, and is now a preflight inside two skills. Everything else accreted around it, with two branching profiles so the ceremony scales to the work and two audited carve-outs so the process does not grow silent workarounds.

Two of those rules do more work than the rest. Merged is not accepted — every issue whose fix lands on the integration branch is labeled into a visible human verification queue, and automatic closing keywords are banned, so closing an issue is always a human decision made against real data. And versioning separates cadence from magnitude: a round of work triggers a bump, the highest-impact change in it sizes the bump, and no bump happens without my confirmation.

The agent layer — where the primitives were proven

The third layer is the smallest, and the easiest to oversell. It is a deliberately small Python scaffold, around six hundred lines with roughly two fifths of that being documentation. It exposes five tools to the model — create a project, create a task, list team members, read a schema, query tasks — and none of them is destructive. There is no delete and no update, and the documented bar for adding one is a confirmation step in code, not in the prompt.

It is frozen, and that is the honest description of it. Its last behavioral change was ten hours after it was built. It is not the engine the system runs on; it is where the primitives — the tool boundary, the model routing, the cost visibility, the secret handling — were worked out in real code before graduating into policy that governs every surface.

06 · Supervision

How do you know what it did?

For a while, I did not. The connector authenticated as me, so every page the AI created and every edit it made was stamped with my name. Those pages sat in the workspace beside my own work, indistinguishable from it. The platform exposes no per-property edit attribution, so that history cannot be sorted out now and cannot be sorted out later. Whatever was written then is permanently ambiguous.

That is the origin of the rule, and I would rather state it that way than present the design as foresight. The AI now has its own account, a member with a name of its own: Main. Every page, edit and comment it makes carries that name in the platform's audit fields — written by the platform rather than claimed by the AI, so it is not a convention that can be forgotten in a session.

Three questions, three separate answers

The obvious simplification, and the one the rulebook explicitly defends against, is to collapse authorship, completion and ownership into a single “who touched this” field. Each carries information the other two do not.

The three provenance questions, the channel that answers each, who is permitted to write it, and why the three are kept separate.
The question Where the answer lives Who may write it Why it stays separate
Who created this? The platform's system-managed authorship field The platform. Neither the AI nor I may touch it. Authorship is not completion. A task I create and the AI finishes still reads as created by me, and that is correct.
Who did the work? A status split — an AI-complete state standing beside my own done state The AI, on work it actually did It is a provenance marker, not a review queue. AI-complete means complete, and a project whose tasks are all AI-complete is a finished project.
Who owns this? Assignment Me, only. The AI never assigns itself. I use assignment-to-the-AI as my own marker of AI-completed work — and it carries information only because the AI never sets it. A human-only channel, preserved by prohibition.
What did it do, and when? A timestamped comment on every status transition The AI, on every transition, without exception The status says that it acted. The comment says what it did. A silent status flip is an incomplete transition.

The third row is the one people find strange, and it is my favorite rule in the system. The AI is forbidden from assigning work to itself. That prohibition is not modesty, it is signal design: because the AI never sets that field, I can use it as my own private marker and it stays meaningful forever. A channel is only readable if something is reliably not writing to it.

Anti-theater

Two rules exist because the AI did the thing once and I caught it.

The first: completion is an objective test, not a feeling. Before anything is marked AI-complete, every checkbox in the task body has to be checked, and the AI may not widen its own scope to clear the gate. Out of scope or blocked means the task stops at in-progress and the exit comment names what is outstanding. A silent stop is exactly the moment I need to be told why.

The second: backfilling is forbidden. Creating a task after the work is done and stamping it through the lifecycle in one pass makes the status cosmetic — the workspace never showed the in-progress state while the work was happening, so nobody could have observed it. The rulebook is blunter than I am: a live status no one could have observed is theater.

Audit from either end

The two surfaces are wired to each other, which makes the system auditable by two very different people without either learning the other's tools.

Start from the project record: a task carries its issue, the pull request that closed it out, its status history and the AI's timestamped comments, and you arrive at the diff. Or start from the diff: a branch names its issue, the issue carries its labels and its verification state, and the trail leads back to the task and the project it serves. You arrive at the reason it was done.

Nobody has to know which system to ask first. A director audits a project in the workspace as easily as a developer audits code in the repository, and the two end up looking at the same facts — who did what, when, and why. The trail is built as a console for whoever is running the project, not as a log for management to read over their shoulder.

The gates are built to stop the system's own mistakes before they ship. At real volume, some will get through anyway — so the second design goal is that putting one back is a prompt, not an archaeology project.

So the correction is built for as deliberately as the gate.

Implementers do not sign off on themselves

The last piece of supervision is not human. A different vendor's model — Codex — reviews the rules and the skills, on the principle that whoever did the work should not verify it. Its raw findings, the reconciliation of each one, and the split between what was executed and what was deferred are all committed rather than summarized.

The one release that shipped with its external review gate waived says so in the changelog: by whom, why, and with the gate re-affirmed. A waived gate that is recorded is a governed exception. A waived gate that is not recorded is just a rule people have stopped following.

07 · Confidentiality

Whose data is it?

The question every leader asks right after supervision, and the one place a promise in a prompt is worth nothing. Three controls carry it.

  1. The account is the privacy boundary. The AI sees what its own account has been explicitly given and nothing else — enforced by the platform's membership model, server-side. A client space that has not been shared is not merely off-limits, it is structurally invisible; and because that failure is quiet, the rule runs both ways: grant access deliberately, and when something seems missing, suspect permissions first. Which account is bound is then verified before the AI's first write of a session, and a mismatch is a hard stop — filed as a bug rather than a documentation change, because the earlier rule assumed the identity was right and let the write proceed when it could not be confirmed. The hazard is real: re-authorizing the connector while a browser is signed in as me silently rebinds every subsequent AI write to my identity, and reports success while doing it. What leaves that boundary is enumerated, including the item nobody enjoys stating: workspace content the agent queries enters model context.
  2. Secrets never touch code. Credentials live in an environment file version control ignores, read through one documented chokepoint. That matters commercially rather than hygienically: it is what makes the work deliverable. A project you cannot hand to a client without first auditing it for leaked keys is a project you do not really own.
  3. The blast radius is bounded structurally, not verbally. The agent layer exposes no destructive tool, so the model cannot delete or overwrite — not because it was told not to, but because the capability does not exist in the code. And anything pointed near real client data is sandbox-first: a throwaway workspace, watched, before anything that matters. Protection expressed as ordering beats protection expressed as policy.
08 · Setup

A governance system only its author can operate is a hobby

Three properties decide whether this one travels. All three assume an operator who is a person — usually the project manager — and who defines the shape the system works in rather than receiving it.

The correct shape is the default shape. Templates carry the structure so nobody has to remember it, the skills make the conventions self-applying, and the rulebook is written to be read two ways at once — by a person, and loaded by an AI as standing instructions. Setup steps carry their reason rather than just their command, and the one hard failure the agent layer can produce tells you the fix instead of printing a stack trace.

Thin pointers, one source of truth. The most repeated meta-rule in the system, and the one that took the most damage to learn. Every surface that needs the rules — coding sessions, desktop, web, mobile, the workspace's own built-in AI — holds a pointer to the canonical document, never a copy.

Replication is configuration, not code. Once the pattern works for one client it works for the next with a new config file and new database identifiers. Nothing about a second client requires writing software, which is what turns a personal practice into something transferable.

Sidebar

The rule that had to be learned twice

The first set of skills was written outside version control. They were lost — not recoverable on any machine — and by the time that was discovered they had already drifted out of step with the documents they rendered.

The rebuilt set was copied to a second workstation, which seemed sensible. That copy fell weeks behind and ran an outdated rule for two weeks with no symptom at all. A wrong rule executing silently is worse than a missing one, because a missing one announces itself.

Both incidents produced the same rule, which is how I know it is the right one: pointers, not copies, plus a setup verification pass that treats drift as a reportable failure rather than something to fix quietly. I want to see the failure before it is papered over.

09 · The build

The system was built by running it on itself

I started this in November 2025, and for the first stretch it was a practice rather than a product: I worked with the AI inside the workspace, corrected it when it went wrong, and had it write down the correction. The repositories came later, in July 2026, and what they did was formalize something already running for months. The dates in the commit history are when the rules were written down, not when they started being true.

Underneath is twenty years of writing code, running repositories and managing projects, which is what decides whether the rules are the right rules. Anyone can generate a best-practice list. What two decades gives you is the knowledge of which practices quietly stop being followed under deadline pressure — and those are the ones that need a mechanism rather than a paragraph.

The method is a loop. I demonstrate a convention live. The AI records the observation. The observation becomes a numbered rule. The rule is compiled into a skill so it applies itself in every future session, and a validator guards its copies against each other. Over all of it sits one standing instruction: ask before assuming.

Every rule has an incident behind it

Nearly every rule carries the date it was captured and the thing that went wrong to produce it. That is not sentimentality about process; it is what stops a rule being deleted by someone — including me, later — who cannot see what it is for. The table below is that history. One row is worth telling properly, because it turns on a single word.

One word. A draft of the rulebook described AI-completed work as “pending review”. I corrected it with a single word — wrong — and that correction is in the record, because it matters more than it looks. If AI-complete means “not really done”, every AI-completed task is outstanding work in someone's mental queue, the provenance marker becomes a backlog, and the whole point of the split evaporates.

10 · The change

The role, re-divided

If you have a project manager, this is theirs. Not a tool installed alongside them and not a monitoring layer over them — a command center they operate. The record-keeping half runs under their supervision, at their instruction, in the shape they define, and every action it takes is attributed and narrated back to them.

What returns to them is the week they were spending on the record, and it goes where it always should have gone: relationships, foresight, and the vertical communication that decides whether a project is merely delivered or actually good. The complaint they have been making for years turns out to have been a specification.

In an organization that has never had a project manager, the role becomes carryable. A CTO, a delivery-minded manager or a solo developer can hold it, because the half that was consuming the hours is no longer theirs to do by hand. And because every action is attributed, timestamped and explained, the role can be shared across the people who each own a piece of the work, instead of living undocumented in one exhausted person's head — which is the version that fails hardest when that person takes a holiday.

Either way, the human keeps the decisions. Every gate that matters here is human-gated on purpose: the round boundary, every version bump, closing any issue, the release sign-off. Nothing important closes without a person deciding it has.

What it does not do

  • Not a replacement

    It does not replace a project manager and it is not a headcount argument. It takes the half of the role nobody became a project manager to do.

  • Not an engine

    The Python layer is a teaching-grade scaffold, deliberately frozen once its ideas graduated into governance. Describing it as a production automation platform would be a claim the artifact cannot support.

  • Not infallible

    The gates are built to stop mistakes before they ship. Some will get through anyway. The design goal is not zero errors — it is that every change is small, attributed, linked across both surfaces and reversible with one instruction.

  • Not measured

    There is no instrumented before-and-after study behind this page. Every number on it is an artifact count, and a delivery-outcome claim is one I have not earned the right to make.

Running this in your organization

Want the Wolfpack AI Command system for your organization? Integration takes hours, not days — mostly installing dependencies, configuring the AI, and adapting the rulebook to how your team already runs projects, which Claude, prompted, does most of the work on. The upgraded workspace can be developed safely in parallel, in isolation from your existing Notion (or other) project-management system, so nothing your team relies on today is touched while it is being built.

Consolidation Under Pressure

Music has never generated more money. The people who build the instruments have never captured less of it. Ten years of transactions explain why — and two economic shocks, not a product problem, explain the wreckage of the last eighteen months.

About this document

This is a market-intelligence report on mergers and acquisitions in music gear and pro audio, written for the executive committee of a music-technology manufacturer and reproduced here in full. Every figure in it is drawn from a public source — company disclosures, regulator case registers, trade-association reporting and trade press — and it contains no confidential information.

The method is the part worth looking at. Forty-three transactions were assembled into a single dated record, each one carrying a link to the filing, press release or report it rests on. Thirty-one of them have a primary source. The other twelve do not, and the table says so in the same column rather than in a caveat at the end — because the reader who needs to know how much weight a number will carry is looking at the number, not at the appendix.

The same record is then plotted as a transaction map, so a decade of activity can be read as a shape: where the money went, when it stopped, and which lane of the industry each deal actually landed in. The conclusions in Parts Five through Eight are drawn from that record, and the methodology at the end states the seven places where the record is thin.

Part zero · Executive summary

What the deal record shows

  1. The premise most of this industry carries is wrong. Recorded music did not collapse — it is at an all-time high of $31.7bn (≈€28.8bn) globally, up 6.4% in 2025, an eleventh consecutive growth year. Physical formats grew 8.0%; vinyl grew 13.7%. What stalled is gear: US music-products retail fell 0.8% to $8.2bn (≈€7.5bn) while the industry absorbed a $627m (≈€570m) tariff bill.
  2. Two macro shocks, not a demand problem, drive the 2024–26 distress. A pandemic surge pulled years of purchases into 2020–21 — an estimated 16 million first-time instrument buyers and the industry's biggest year on record — and then reverted. On top of that, April 2025 tariffs took Chinese musical-instrument duties from ~11% to 145%. Sponsors who underwrote 2021 revenue as a run-rate were underwriting a spike.
  3. Native Instruments is the clearest casualty, and it was a balance-sheet failure. Preliminary insolvency 27 January 2026 — then three separate buyers took the estate inside six months: inMusic (NI core, May), Boris FX (iZotope, July), and founder Dirk Ulrich (Plugin Alliance + Brainworx, July). Assets nobody wants do not sell three times in ninety days.
  4. The outsider-buyer trade is dead. Masimo −$680m on Sound United; Etsy −$170m on Reverb; Sonova exiting Sennheiser Consumer after four years. Three non-audio acquirers, three losses.
  5. A regulator has drawn a market definition around DJ equipment. New Zealand's Commerce Commission blocked AlphaTheta/Serato on 18 July 2024. That is precedent, and it binds the category leader.
  6. Two disruptions the deal record does not capture: subscription bundles that put a studio's worth of plugins at $15–30 (≈€13–26) a month, and AI tools now used by 78% of professional musicians — both of which reduce the amount of hardware a working creator needs to buy.

The platform tier, for scale

Spotify reported 300 million Premium subscribers in Q2 2026 — the first audio service to reach that mark — on 777 million monthly actives (+12%), quarterly revenue of €4.78bn (≈$5.52bn), up 14%, a record 33.4% gross margin, and €545m (≈$629m) of net income against an €86m (≈$99m) loss a year earlier. YouTube Music and Premium passed 125 million subscribers in March 2025, up from 100m in February 2024, and paid the music industry $8bn (≈€6.9bn) in the twelve months to June 2025.

Spotify earned €545m (≈$629m) of net income in a single quarter — comparable to the $627m (≈€570m) tariff bill the entire US music-products industry absorbed over a full year.

What actually happened, correctly stated

The popular framing — streaming boomed, physical died, gear died with it — is wrong in two of its three clauses.

  • Recorded music revenue boomed. Eleven straight years of growth to an all-time high.
  • Physical recorded music recovered. 2025 was only the second year on record in which physical growth outpaced digital. CDs keep declining; vinyl has more than replaced them in value, passing $1bn (≈€0.9bn) in the US on 46.8m units against 29.5m CDs.
  • Gear flattened, then went sideways with a tax on top. NAMM calls 2025 “a year of stabilization.” Read against −0.8% retail and a $627m tariff bill, that means running to stand still.

The real dynamic is not decline. It is value migration. Music's economics moved from ownership to access, and access accrues to rights-holders and platforms. Hardware sits at the least monetizable point in the chain: bought once, by a creator population growing in headcount while spending less per head.

Note

The counter-signal, which matters

Gear is not uniformly dead — it is bifurcating. NAMM's decade view puts fretted instruments up 38% and the US synth / DJ / electronic category up 36.5%, with live touring continuing to expand. Growth concentrates in the categories tied to electronic creation and live performance: the input content platforms need, and the experience they cannot deliver.

Part two

The two economic shocks that produced the distress

Nearly every failure in the 2024–26 record traces to one of two macro events, and in the worst cases to both. Neither is a product problem or a competitive one.

Shock 1 · The pandemic pulled years of demand into eighteen months

Lockdowns plus stimulus payments produced the largest demand spike in the industry's modern history. 2021 was the music-products industry's biggest year on record. Electric guitar sales rose roughly 30% — a larger move than the preceding decade combined — acoustic guitars nearly 15%, on an estimated 16 million first-time buyers. Fender and Gibson both reported record 2020 sales; Fender said it had never seen a year like it. Software moved the same way: the pandemic gave plugin and DAW sales a step-change in 2020–21.

The problem is the shape, not the size. Sixteen million first-time buyers in a lockdown window is a cohort, not a run-rate. Instruments and plugins are durable; a buyer acquired in 2020 is not in the market again in 2022. When the spike reverted, the industry did not face a normal downturn — it faced the absence of demand it had already served.

Caution

Why this matters for the deal record

Every major sponsor entry in music software happened at or immediately after the peak: Francisco Partners took majority control of Native Instruments in 2021 and added Plugin Alliance and Brainworx in April 2022. Those prices were underwritten against spike revenue, and the leverage placed on top of them assumed that revenue would persist. It did not, and debt service is indifferent to the reason.

Shock 2 · The 2025 tariffs taxed a market that had already stopped growing

On 2 April 2025 a universal 10% US import tariff was announced, effective 5 April, with reciprocal rates of 10–50% across 60 countries. By 12 April 2025, musical-instrument products from China carried a 145% duty — against an average of roughly 11% the year before. China had supplied $560m (≈€509m), or 35.7%, of US musical-instrument and parts imports in 2024.

  • 80% of manufacturers surveyed said they would raise prices within six months, by 5% to 100% or more.
  • The industry absorbed $627m (≈€570m) in tariff cost across 2025.
  • Combined revenue for the 100 largest US suppliers rose 1.9% to ~$10.5bn (≈€9.5bn) — “entirely due to tariff related price increases.”
  • US musical-instrument exports for January–July 2025 ran 8.4% below the 2021–24 average at $491.3m (≈€447m) — the worst year since 2020 — with retaliation and uncertainty hitting Brazil (−28.3%), China (−27.1%) and Italy (−22.7%).

The supplier tier's only growth in 2025 was a tax pass-through. There was no real volume growth anywhere in the US channel.

The honest limit of this argument. Tariffs are a duty on imported goods; they did not touch the cost base of a German software company directly. The transmission to software is indirect but real — a US creator market absorbing double-digit hardware price increases has less discretionary budget, and the retail channel that merchandises plugins alongside gear contracted with it. Attribute the hardware damage to tariffs directly, and the software damage to the pandemic reversion first, tariff-depressed US discretionary spend second.

Part four

The three transactions that define the category

1 · AlphaTheta / Pioneer DJ — three owners, one brand, and a regulator

The chain of custody. Pioneer Corporation sold its DJ division to KKR in 2014 for a reported ~$551m (≈€414m). On 1 January 2020 the entity renamed itself AlphaTheta Corporation; two months later KKR and Pioneer sold their stakes to Noritsu Koki, a Japanese holding company with origins in photo-processing equipment, for a reported ~$606m (≈€532m). In January 2024 products began shipping under the AlphaTheta name alongside Pioneer DJ.

So: a purchase, and then a rebrand on top of it. Brand equity stayed with “Pioneer DJ” while corporate identity moved to AlphaTheta — which is why the market read the 2024 product rebrand as a strategy change when it was the completion of a four-year-old corporate one. AlphaTheta holds a reported ~70% share of the global DJ equipment market.

The regulatory event. In July 2023 AlphaTheta announced it would acquire Serato. New Zealand's Commerce Commission issued a Statement of Preliminary Issues in October 2023 and, on 18 July 2024, declined clearance — finding the merger would give AlphaTheta dominance in DJ hardware and DJ software and substantially lessen competition. The deal was abandoned.

Why this matters more than the deal itself. A competition authority has formally defined “DJ hardware” and “DJ software” as markets and intervened. That analysis is available to every other regulator. For every competitor in the category, an independent Serato is now a regulatory settlement — not a commercial accident.

2 · Etsy / Reverb — the most instructive value destruction in the set

Etsy acquired Reverb in 2019 for $275m (≈€246m). It agreed to sell on 21 April 2025; the transaction closed 2 June 2025 at $105m (≈€95m) in cash, with Etsy recording a $5.1m loss on sale. That is a 62% reduction in nominal value over six years — −$170m (≈−€154m).

The buyers are the point. Reverb was acquired by affiliates of Servco Pacific — majority owner of Fender Musical Instruments — and Creator Partners, founded by former SoundCloud CEO Kerry Trainor.

  • Marketplace economics in gear are worse than they look. High-consideration, low-frequency, high-shipping-cost, high-authentication-cost. Etsy's core competency did not transfer.
  • Nobody valued the data asset separately. Reverb's Price Guide covers 240,000+ products and records completed transaction prices; its Price Index is the closest thing this industry has to a Manheim or Kelley Blue Book. No carve-out was attempted.
  • The neutral-referee seat is now vacant. With Fender's owner controlling the largest gear transaction dataset, no competing brand can treat Reverb's published indices as disinterested.

3 · Native Instruments — a balance-sheet failure, broken up three ways

How it broke. Francisco Partners took majority control of Native Instruments in 2021 — the peak year — and assembled Soundwide, adding iZotope and, in April 2022, Plugin Alliance and Brainworx from founder Dirk Ulrich. Reporting around the insolvency describes declining sales and accumulated losses under sponsor ownership, with debt service compounding the pressure. On 27 January 2026 Native Instruments GmbH and Native Instruments Group entered preliminary insolvency proceedings in Germany.

What the evidence supports, and what it does not. The pandemic gave software sales a step-change in 2020–21; when the boost ended, sales fell back and the companies that had taken on debt against those revenues could not carry it — Native Instruments and payments processor Digital River are the named casualties of that pattern. That is a capital-structure failure under a reverting demand curve, compounded by a US market absorbing 145% instrument tariffs. There is no evidence in the public record of customer defection to a competitor, of a product-quality failure, or of a technology shift away from NI's categories. Kontakt remains the industry-standard sampler and Traktor a top-tier DJ platform.

Note

The strongest evidence that the products were fine

Three separate buyers took the estate inside six months, in coordinated transactions:

inMusic → Native Instruments core (Kontakt, Traktor, Maschine) and the 25m-registered-user base, definitive agreement 7 May 2026.
Boris FX → iZotope (RX, Ozone, Neutron, Nectar), announced 2 July 2026.
Dirk Ulrich / RCKFRC → Plugin Alliance and Brainworx repurchased from Francisco Partners on 15 July 2026, joining Manley Labs and Apogee Electronics; Winni Schapitz returns as Brainworx CEO.

Assets nobody wants do not clear three times in ninety days — and a founder does not buy back a business he believes is broken.

Two consequences worth board attention.

  1. inMusic bought the user base, not the portfolio. What remained after the split is the sampler, the DJ platform, and 25 million direct customer relationships — a distribution channel for every hardware product inMusic sells, not replicable at any price. The plugin and mastering brands went elsewhere. Approximately 100 roles were cut in July 2026 and NI's UK office reportedly closed.
  2. The most valuable AI-audio asset in the estate left the music industry. iZotope — whose RX and Ozone are the reference AI-assisted repair and mastering tools — now sits inside a visual-effects company. Its CEO framed the deal around serving creators across “everything they hear and see.” Music-hardware incumbents did not win that asset; an adjacent-media software house did.
Part five

Five patterns in the deal record

Pattern 1 · The outsider-buyer trade is dead

Three non-audio strategics bought major audio assets. All three exited at a loss within four years.

The strategic logic was legible on a slide in every case — adjacency in sound, in marketplaces, in acoustics — and wrong in operation. The pool of credible buyers has contracted to industry strategics and sponsors already inside the sector. Price expectations built on a 2019–2022 comparable set should be revised down.

Pattern 2 · The private-equity software thesis broke on timing, not on logic

Sponsors entered music software on a thesis of recurring revenue, high gross margin and subscription conversion. The thesis was not wrong — subscription is exactly where the category went. The entry timing was wrong. Francisco Partners bought into Native Instruments in 2021 and added Plugin Alliance in April 2022; STG took Avid private at $1.4bn (≈€1.30bn) in November 2023 on debt from Sixth Street and Silver Point. Every one of those prices was set against pandemic-inflated revenue.

The structural error was pacing: software multiples underwritten against software-industry growth curves, in a sector whose demand had just been pulled forward by two years and whose customers were about to face double-digit hardware price increases.

Pattern 3 · Distress has replaced auction as the source of quality deal flow

The best assets transacted in the last two years came out of an insolvency — and were split three ways. Guitar Center spent August 2025 pushing a January 2026 maturity wall out to 2029. Sennheiser Consumer is on the market for the second time in four years.

Operational implication: an acquisition capability built for auction processes — bankers, data rooms, competitive tension — is the wrong capability for this market. What matters is moving inside a 90-day administrator timetable with committed funding and a pre-formed integration plan. inMusic went from insolvency filing to definitive agreement in roughly fourteen weeks; Boris FX and Dirk Ulrich closed their pieces within ten more.

Pattern 4 · Competition regulators now have a view on this industry

The NZCC's July 2024 decision is the first time an authority has defined and assessed concentration in DJ hardware and DJ software as distinct markets. That is on the record and available to every other agency.

The second-order effect is more interesting: regulatory intervention has become a competitive input. Serato's independence was preserved by a regulator, not by a market. Every DJ-hardware manufacturer partnering with independent software now does so under a protection it did not negotiate and cannot rely on indefinitely.

Pattern 5 · Consolidation runs along the signal chain, not within categories

Nobody is buying more of what they already make. Every significant acquirer is buying adjacency.

Caution

The counter-force, and it is winning in software

Signal-chain integration is the pattern in hardware, where a group can amortize distribution, dealers and certification across categories. It is not the pattern in software, where 2026 ran the opposite way: the largest portfolio ever assembled could not be sold whole and cleared only in three pieces, to three focused owners. Read Patterns 5 and the break-up implication in Part Eight together — breadth is consolidating in hardware and fragmenting in software, and a company holding both needs a different answer for each.

Part six

Streaming is not a side topic. It is the demand-side explanation.

The mechanism. Streaming and social distribution enlarged the creator population dramatically while lowering the capital required per creator.

A phone plus a subscription DAW plus one interface now clears a bar that once required a studio.

That is excellent for participation, for platforms, and for rights-holders — and structurally difficult for anyone selling durable hardware, because the marginal new creator buys less equipment than the creator they replaced, and buys it once.

The industry's total addressable market did not shrink. It moved one layer up the stack, into a subscription your customers now pay every month before they consider buying anything from you.

Part seven

Two disruptions the deal record does not capture

Neither shows up as a transaction, and both change how much hardware a working creator needs to buy.

Disruption 1 · Subscription collapsed the price of a studio

A plugin collection that cost thousands of dollars to assemble under perpetual licenses is now rented for the price of two streaming subscriptions. The shift is not partial — Waves has stopped selling individual plugins entirely and moved to subscription-only.

  • The hardware consequence is direct. Compressors, EQs, channel strips, tape emulations and reverbs that were once bought as outboard units are now line items in a $15–30 (≈€13–26) monthly bundle. Every hour of that workflow is an hour not spent buying, racking or replacing hardware.
  • The commercial-studio consequence is worse. The historical reason to book a room was access to equipment no individual could afford. Subscription removed that reason for most of the mid-tier work that used to fill those rooms.
  • The industry ate its own margin. Slate's Complete Access is bundled free for three months with Solid State Logic interfaces — a hardware maker (both owned by Audiotonix) using a plugin subscription as a hardware inducement, which is precisely the inversion of the model plugins were originally sold on.
Caution

The irony worth naming

Plugin Alliance's subscription tiers helped establish the price point that made perpetual-license plugin businesses hard to sustain. Francisco Partners bought Plugin Alliance in April 2022 at the top of the market it had helped reprice; four years later the founder bought it back out of the insolvency estate.

Disruption 2 · AI moved work from engineers into software

AI in music production is no longer prospective. In a 2026 study by Water & Music and Moises, 78% of professional musicians reported using AI for music-related work in the previous twelve months, against 60% of hobbyists — professionals are leading adoption, not resisting it. Vocal isolation led task adoption at 71%. A separate 2026 Sonarworks survey of 1,100+ producers found 60% using AI as an ideation tool and 30% as a co-producer whose suggestions reach the finished track.

The tasks AI has absorbed are precisely the ones that used to be billed by the hour: stem separation, noise repair, reference-matching, mastering, vocal tuning, smart EQ, and level balancing.

  • Mastering is the clearest displacement. LANDR, eMastered, BandLab Mastering and CloudBounce now dominate the volume tier. LANDR's own positioning targets creators who release frequently and need masters “without involving a mastering engineer.” Flagship major-label work still goes to humans; the indie and mid-tier work that sustained most mastering practices does not.
  • It takes share from software as well as services. An AI assistant that sets a chain in seconds reduces the number of specialist plugins a user feels they need — which is why AI features are now a defensive requirement for every plugin vendor, not a differentiator.
  • And it takes share from hardware. The pitch for an outboard channel strip was always expertise embodied in a circuit. The pitch for an AI assistant is expertise embodied in software, at zero marginal cost, inside the DAW the customer already owns.

The strategic tell: the industry's leading AI-audio asset — iZotope, maker of RX and Ozone — was acquired in July 2026 by Boris FX, a visual-effects software company. No music-hardware incumbent won it.

Note

Confidence note

The adoption percentages above come from vendor- and media-published surveys with self-selected samples (Sonarworks, n>1,100; Water & Music / Moises). They are directionally reliable and consistent with each other, but they are not audited panel data and should not be quoted as precise market measurements. No independent syndicated tracker of AI adoption in music production exists.

Part eight

Implications for an acquisitive hardware-and-software manufacturer

  1. What you bought is a user base, and that is the right thing to have bought. After the three-way split, the acquired asset is Kontakt, Traktor, Maschine and 25 million direct relationships. Govern on retention of that base through the first two product cycles, not on cost synergy — the plugin and mastering brands that would have justified a portfolio thesis went to other buyers.
  2. Do not plan against a recovering hardware market. 2025's only supplier-level revenue growth was tariff pass-through, and US exports ran 8.4% below their four-year average. Any 2027 plan assuming volume recovery assumes a reversal no data here supports.
  3. Cut the tail. Own a narrow vertical rather than a wide net at many price points. The case for a broad catalog was operating leverage: one distribution network, one dealer relationship, many SKUs. Tariffs destroyed that logic — a 145% duty is levied per unit, so breadth now multiplies exposure instead of amortizing it, and every marginal SKU carries inventory, tooling, certification and support cost against a category that is not growing. The categories with real decade growth are narrow and specific (US synth/DJ/electronic +36.5%, fretted +38%). Depth in one of those beats presence in ten. Assume the portfolio review has to be brutal: the long tail is not optionality, it is carrying cost.
  4. Scale has become a liability where it used to be the moat — expect conglomerates to break up, not bulk up. The 2026 record already shows it. The Native Instruments estate did not sell as a portfolio; it cleared only once it was split three ways to three focused owners. iZotope went to a company that does one thing well in an adjacent medium. Plugin Alliance and Brainworx went back to their founder — a small, family-held owner operating alongside Manley and Apogee. Sonova is unwinding Sennheiser Consumer; Masimo unwound Sound United; Bose divested its professional division. Every large diversified owner in this report that assembled breadth has since sold it, and the buyers who made money were the focused ones.
  5. For anyone selling software into subscription, lean is the only viable structure. Subscription converts a lumpy, high-margin license business into a thin recurring one, and a thin recurring business cannot carry conglomerate overhead or sponsor leverage — that is precisely the arithmetic that ended Soundwide. A founder-run operation with a small team can survive at $15–30 (≈€13–26) per user per month; a private-equity-owned roll-up servicing acquisition debt on the same revenue cannot. The structural implication is uncomfortable but clear: in plugins, the efficient operating size is small, and the July 2026 founder buy-back is the market demonstrating it.
  6. Price the subscription question before a competitor does. At $15–30 (≈€13–26) a month for 100–220 plugins, the willingness to pay for perpetual software has been reset permanently. A hardware business that bundles software has an advantage here that a pure software business does not — SSL/Slate is already running that play.
  7. Treat AI as table stakes in software and as a threat in hardware. 78% professional adoption means an AI-assisted workflow is now the default context your hardware is evaluated in. The question is not whether to add AI features but which hardware categories survive a customer whose DAW already does the job.
  8. Serato's independence is a regulatory artifact, not a durable market condition. It depends on the category leader not finding a structure a regulator will accept.
  9. DJ and electronic is the growth exception — and the leader holds ~70%. +36.5% US decade growth in a flat market makes this the most attractive and most concentrated category in the industry.
  10. Rebuild deal capability around distress, not auction — standing committed capital, a pre-cleared integration playbook, and legal capacity in German and UK insolvency. 2026 demonstrated that speed, not price, wins these processes.
  11. The market-data seat is open. Reverb's dataset sits under a competitor's owner; MI SalesTrak is a small independent panel; NAMM and Music Trades publish annually without published methodology; no one measures AI adoption or subscription substitution at all. There is no neutral, timely, transparent measure of this market.

Known gaps

  1. No transaction multiples. Almost no deal in this sector discloses revenue or EBITDA multiples. Every valuation inference here is drawn from entry-versus-exit price on the three outsider-buyer cases — the only place the arithmetic is public.
  2. US market sizing depends on two paywalled annual sources (Music Trades Census, NAMM Global Report), neither of which publishes methodology or error bounds. The $8.2bn and −0.8% figures are cited as reported.
  3. No DJ-category revenue series exists. DJ equipment has no dedicated commodity code and no syndicated tracker.
  4. The tariff-to-software transmission is inferred, not measured. No public dataset isolates US plugin or DAW spending, so the claim that tariff-compressed discretionary budgets contributed to software distress is reasoned from adjacency, not demonstrated.
  5. Subscription substitution is unquantified. No source measures how much hardware purchasing subscription bundles displace. The direction is clear; the magnitude is not established.
  6. Private-company financials are unavailable for inMusic, AlphaTheta, Audiotonix, Sweetwater, and Guitar Center beyond disclosed debt events.
  7. Announced transactions only. Processes that failed before announcement, and minority investments below disclosure thresholds, are invisible here.

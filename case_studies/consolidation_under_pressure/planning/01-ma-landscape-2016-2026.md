## Executive summary

1. **The premise most of this industry carries is wrong.** Recorded music did not collapse;
   it is at an all-time high of **$31.7bn (≈€28.8bn) globally, up 6.4% in 2025** — an
   eleventh consecutive growth year. Physical formats *grew* 8.0%; vinyl grew 13.7%. What
   stalled is **gear**: US music-products retail fell 0.8% to **$8.2bn (≈€7.5bn)** while the
   industry absorbed a **$627m (≈€570m) tariff bill**.

2. **Two macro shocks, not a demand problem, drive the 2024–26 distress.** The pandemic
   pulled years of purchases into 2020–21 — an estimated **16 million first-time instrument
   buyers**, the industry's biggest year on record — and then reverted. On top of that, April
   2025 tariffs took Chinese musical-instrument duties from ~11% to **145%**. Sponsors who
   underwrote 2021 revenue as a run-rate were underwriting a spike.

3. **Native Instruments is the clearest casualty, and it was a balance-sheet failure.**
   Preliminary insolvency 27 January 2026 — then **three separate buyers took the estate
   inside six months**: inMusic (NI core, May), Boris FX (iZotope, July), and founder Dirk
   Ulrich (Plugin Alliance + Brainworx, July). Assets nobody wants do not sell three times in
   ninety days.

4. **The outsider-buyer trade is dead.** Masimo −$680m on Sound United; Etsy −$170m on
   Reverb; Sonova exiting Sennheiser Consumer after four years.

5. **A regulator has drawn a market definition around DJ equipment.** New Zealand's Commerce
   Commission blocked AlphaTheta/Serato on 18 July 2024. That is precedent, and it binds the
   category leader.

6. **Two disruptions the deal record does not capture:** subscription bundles that put a
   studio's worth of plugins at **$15–30 (≈€13–26) a month**, and AI tools now used by **78%
   of professional musicians** — both of which reduce the amount of hardware a working
   creator needs to buy.

### What actually happened, correctly stated

The popular framing — streaming boomed, physical died, gear died with it — is wrong in two
of its three clauses.

- **Recorded music revenue boomed.** Eleven straight years of growth to an all-time high.
- **Physical recorded music recovered.** 2025 was only the second year on record in which
  physical growth outpaced digital. CDs keep declining; vinyl has more than replaced them.
- **Gear flattened, then went sideways with a tax on top.** NAMM calls 2025 "a year of
  stabilization." Read against −0.8% retail and a $627m tariff bill, that means running to
  stand still.

The real dynamic is **value migration**. Music's economics moved from ownership to access,
and access accrues to rights-holders and platforms. Hardware sits at the least monetizable
point in the chain: bought once, by a creator population growing in headcount while spending
less per head.

**The counter-signal.** Gear is bifurcating, not dying. NAMM's decade view puts **fretted
instruments up 38%** and the **US synth / DJ / electronic category up 36.5%**, with live
touring continuing to expand.

## Part II — The two economic shocks that produced the distress

Nearly every failure in the 2024–26 record traces to one of two macro events, and in the
worst cases to both. Neither is a product problem or a competitive one.

### Shock 1 · The pandemic pulled years of demand into eighteen months

Lockdowns plus stimulus payments produced the largest demand spike in the industry's modern
history. **2021 was the music-products industry's biggest year on record.** Electric guitar
sales rose roughly 30% — a larger move than the preceding decade combined — acoustic guitars
nearly 15%, on an estimated **16 million first-time buyers**. Fender and Gibson both reported
record 2020 sales; Fender said it had never seen a year like it. Software moved the same way:
the pandemic gave plugin and DAW sales a step-change in 2020–21.

**The problem is the shape, not the size.** Sixteen million first-time buyers in a lockdown
window is a *cohort*, not a run-rate. Instruments and plugins are durable; a buyer acquired in
2020 is not in the market again in 2022. When the spike reverted, the industry did not face a
normal downturn — it faced the absence of demand it had already served.

**Why this matters for the deal record.** Every major sponsor entry in music software happened
at or immediately after the peak: Francisco Partners took majority control of Native
Instruments in **2021** and added Plugin Alliance and Brainworx in **April 2022**. Those prices
were underwritten against spike revenue, and the leverage placed on top of them assumed that
revenue would persist. It did not, and debt service is indifferent to the reason.

### Shock 2 · The 2025 tariffs taxed a market that had already stopped growing

On 2 April 2025 a universal 10% US import tariff was announced, effective 5 April, with
reciprocal rates of 10–50% across 60 countries. By **12 April 2025, musical-instrument
products from China carried a 145% duty** — against an average of roughly **11%** the year
before. China had supplied **$560m (≈€509m), or 35.7%, of US musical-instrument and parts
imports in 2024**.

- **80% of manufacturers surveyed said they would raise prices** within six months, by 5% to
  100% or more.
- The industry absorbed **$627m (≈€570m)** in tariff cost across 2025.
- Combined revenue for the 100 largest US suppliers rose 1.9% to ~$10.5bn (≈€9.5bn) —
  **"entirely due to tariff related price increases."**
- US musical-instrument *exports* for January–July 2025 ran **8.4% below the 2021–24 average**
  at $491.3m (≈€447m) — the worst year since 2020 — with declines to Brazil (−28.3%), China
  (−27.1%) and Italy (−22.7%).

> The supplier tier's only growth in 2025 was a tax pass-through. There was no real volume
> growth anywhere in the US channel.

**The honest limit of this argument.** Tariffs are a duty on imported goods; they did not touch
the cost base of a German software company directly. The transmission to software is indirect
but real — a US creator market absorbing double-digit hardware price increases has less
discretionary budget, and the retail channel that merchandises plugins alongside gear
contracted with it. Attribute the hardware damage to tariffs directly, and the software damage
to the pandemic reversion first, tariff-depressed US discretionary spend second.

## Part IV — The three deals in focus

### 1. AlphaTheta / Pioneer DJ — three owners, one brand, and a regulator

**The chain of custody.** Pioneer Corporation sold its DJ division to KKR in 2014 for a
reported ~$551m (≈€414m). On 1 January 2020 the entity renamed itself **AlphaTheta
Corporation**; two months later KKR and Pioneer sold their stakes to **Noritsu Koki**, a
Japanese holding company whose origins are in photo-processing equipment, for a reported
~$606m (≈€532m). In January 2024 products began shipping under the AlphaTheta name alongside
Pioneer DJ.

**So: a purchase, and then a rebrand on top of it.** Brand equity stayed with "Pioneer DJ"
while corporate identity moved to AlphaTheta — which is why the market read the 2024 product
rebrand as a strategy change when it was the completion of a four-year-old corporate one.
AlphaTheta holds a reported **~70% share of the global DJ equipment market**.

**The regulatory event.** In July 2023 AlphaTheta announced it would acquire **Serato**. New
Zealand's Commerce Commission issued a Statement of Preliminary Issues in October 2023 and, on
**18 July 2024, declined clearance** — finding the merger would give AlphaTheta dominance in
DJ hardware and DJ software and "substantially lessen competition." The deal was abandoned.

**Why this matters more than the deal itself.** A competition authority has formally defined
"DJ hardware" and "DJ software" as markets and intervened. That analysis is available to every
other regulator. For every competitor in the category, an independent Serato is now a
regulatory settlement — not a commercial accident.

### 2. Etsy / Reverb — the most instructive value destruction in the set

*The published page carries this as a diagram: 2019 Etsy —$275M→ Reverb; 2025 Etsy —$105M→
Servco Pacific + Creator Partners; Servco Pacific owns Fender; Creator Partners founded by
Kerry Trainor, ex-SoundCloud.*

Etsy acquired Reverb in 2019 for **$275m (≈€246m)**. It agreed to sell on 21 April 2025; the
transaction closed **2 June 2025 at $105m (≈€95m) in cash**, with Etsy recording a $5.1m loss
on sale. That is a **62% reduction in nominal value over six years** — **−$170m (≈−€154m)**.

**The buyers are the point.** Reverb was acquired by affiliates of **Servco Pacific** —
majority owner of Fender Musical Instruments — and **Creator Partners**, founded by former
SoundCloud CEO Kerry Trainor.

- **Marketplace economics in gear are worse than they look.** High-consideration,
  low-frequency, high-shipping-cost, high-authentication-cost. Etsy's core competency did not
  transfer.
- **Nobody valued the data asset separately.** Reverb's Price Guide covers 240,000+ products
  and records completed transaction prices; its Price Index is the closest thing this industry
  has to a Manheim or Kelley Blue Book. No carve-out was attempted.
- **The neutral-referee seat is now vacant.** With Fender's owner controlling the largest gear
  transaction dataset, no competing brand can treat Reverb's published indices as
  disinterested.

### 3. Native Instruments — a balance-sheet failure, broken up three ways

**How it broke.** Francisco Partners took majority control of Native Instruments in **2021** —
the peak year — and assembled **Soundwide**, adding iZotope and, in **April 2022**, Plugin
Alliance and Brainworx from founder Dirk Ulrich. Reporting around the insolvency describes
declining sales and accumulated losses under sponsor ownership, with debt service compounding
the pressure. On **27 January 2026** Native Instruments GmbH and Native Instruments Group
entered **preliminary insolvency proceedings** in Germany.

**What the evidence supports, and what it does not.** The pandemic gave software sales a
step-change in 2020–21; when the boost ended, sales fell back and the companies that had taken
on debt against those revenues could not carry it — Native Instruments and payments processor
Digital River are the named casualties of that pattern. That is a **capital-structure failure
under a reverting demand curve, compounded by a US market absorbing 145% instrument tariffs**.
There is **no evidence in the public record of customer defection to a competitor, of a
product-quality failure, or of a technology shift** away from NI's categories. Kontakt remains
the industry-standard sampler and Traktor a top-tier DJ platform.

**The strongest evidence that the products were fine.** Three separate buyers took the estate
inside six months, in coordinated transactions:

| Buyer | Assets | Date |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **inMusic** | Native Instruments core — Kontakt, Traktor, Maschine — and the **25m-registered-user base** | Definitive agreement **7 May 2026** |
| **Boris FX** | **iZotope** — RX, Ozone, Neutron, Nectar | Announced **2 July 2026** |
| **Dirk Ulrich / RCKFRC** | **Plugin Alliance and Brainworx**, repurchased from Francisco Partners; joins Manley Labs and Apogee Electronics. Winni Schapitz returns as Brainworx CEO | **15 July 2026** |

Assets nobody wants do not clear three times in ninety days — and a founder does not buy back
a business he believes is broken.

**Two consequences worth board attention.**

1. **inMusic bought the user base, not the portfolio.** What remained after the split is the
   sampler, the DJ platform, and 25 million direct customer relationships — a distribution
   channel for every hardware product inMusic sells, not replicable at any price. The plugin
   and mastering brands went elsewhere. Approximately 100 roles were cut in July 2026 and NI's
   UK office reportedly closed.
2. **The most valuable AI-audio asset in the estate left the music industry.** iZotope — whose
   RX and Ozone are the reference AI-assisted repair and mastering tools — now sits inside a
   visual-effects company, framed by its CEO around serving creators across "everything they
   hear and see." Music-hardware incumbents did not win that asset.

## Part V — Five patterns in the deal record

### Pattern 1 — The outsider-buyer trade is dead

| Acquirer | Core business | Asset | In | Out | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Masimo | Medical devices | Sound United | $1.03bn (≈€981m), 2022 | $350m (≈€318m), 2025 | **−$680m (≈−€618m) in 3 years** |
| Etsy | E-commerce marketplace | Reverb | $275m (≈€246m), 2019 | $105m (≈€95m), 2025 | **−$170m (≈−€154m) in 6 years** |
| Sonova | Hearing technology | Sennheiser Consumer | €200m (≈$236m), 2021 | for sale, 2026 | Exit signaled at 4 years |

**The pool of credible buyers has contracted to industry strategics and sponsors already
inside the sector.** Price expectations built on a 2019–2022 comparable set should be revised
down.

### Pattern 2 — The private-equity software thesis broke on timing, not on logic

The thesis — recurring revenue, high gross margin, subscription conversion — was not wrong;
subscription is exactly where the category went. The **entry timing** was wrong. Francisco
Partners bought into NI in 2021 and added Plugin Alliance in April 2022; STG took Avid private
at $1.4bn (≈€1.30bn) in November 2023. Every one of those prices was set against
pandemic-inflated revenue. The structural error was **pacing**: software multiples underwritten
against software-industry growth curves, in a sector whose demand had just been pulled forward
by two years and whose customers were about to face double-digit hardware price increases.

### Pattern 3 — Distress has replaced auction as the source of quality deal flow

The best assets transacted in the last two years came out of an insolvency and were split three
ways. Guitar Center spent August 2025 pushing a January 2026 maturity wall out to 2029.
Sennheiser Consumer is on the market for the second time in four years.

**Operational implication:** an acquisition capability built for auction processes is the wrong
capability. What matters is moving inside a 90-day administrator timetable with committed
funding and a pre-formed integration plan. inMusic went from insolvency filing to definitive
agreement in roughly fourteen weeks; Boris FX and Dirk Ulrich closed their pieces within ten
more.

### Pattern 4 — Competition regulators now have a view on this industry

The NZCC's July 2024 decision is the first time an authority has defined and assessed
concentration in DJ hardware and DJ software as distinct markets. The second-order effect:
**regulatory intervention has become a competitive input.** Serato's independence was preserved
by a regulator, not by a market.

### Pattern 5 — Consolidation runs along the signal chain, not within categories

| Acquirer | Path through the signal chain |
| --- | --- |
| **Audiotonix** | consoles (DiGiCo, Allen & Heath, Calrec, SSL) → immersive (KLANG) → field recording (Sound Devices) → software (Slate, Fourier, Harrison) → **capture** (DPA, Wisycom, Austrian Audio, 2026) |
| **Focusrite** | interfaces → monitors (ADAM) → touring PA (Martin Audio) → synths (Sequential, Oberheim) → amplification (Linea) → plugins (Sonnox) |
| **Fender** | guitars → interfaces and live sound (PreSonus) → **marketplace and price data** (Reverb, via Servco) |
| **Roland** | electronic drums → acoustic drums and percussion (DW, LP, Gretsch, Slingerland) |
| **inMusic** | DJ and controller hardware → synthesis (Moog) → **software and 25m users** (Native Instruments) |
| **Boris FX** | visual effects → **audio repair and mastering** (iZotope) — the first cross-media entrant |

The endpoint is four to six vertically integrated groups controlling the chain from capture to
output, each with a software layer, competing on ecosystem lock-in rather than on individual
products. **There is no stable middle.**

**The counter-force, and it is winning in software.** Signal-chain integration is the pattern in
*hardware*, where a group can amortize distribution, dealers and certification across categories.
It is not the pattern in *software*, where 2026 ran the opposite way: the largest portfolio ever
assembled could not be sold whole and cleared only in three pieces, to three focused owners. Read
this pattern together with implications 3–5 in Part VIII — breadth is consolidating in hardware
and fragmenting in software, and a company holding both needs a different answer for each.

## Part VI — Where the money actually went

| Layer | 2016 position | 2026 position | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Distribution platforms | Spotify pre-profitability | 300m subs, record 33.4% GM, profitable | ▲▲▲ |
| Rights-holders | recovering from a 15-year decline | $31.7bn (≈€28.8bn), 11 straight growth years | ▲▲ |
| Physical media | terminal decline assumed | +8.0%; vinyl +13.7%, 19 straight years | ▲ |
| Live | growing | continuing to expand | ▲ |
| **Tools and instruments** | pre-pandemic baseline | **$8.2bn US retail, −0.8%, $627m tariff cost** | **▬** |

**The mechanism.** Streaming and social distribution enlarged the *creator population*
dramatically while lowering the *capital required per creator*.

> **A phone plus a subscription DAW plus one interface now clears a bar that once required a
> studio.**

That is excellent for participation, for platforms, and for rights-holders — and structurally
difficult for anyone selling durable hardware, because the marginal new creator buys less
equipment than the creator they replaced, and buys it once.

> The industry's total addressable market did not shrink. It moved one layer up the stack, into
> a subscription your customers now pay every month *before* they consider buying anything from
> you.

## Part VII — Two disruptions the deal record does not capture

Neither shows up as a transaction, and both change how much hardware a working creator needs
to buy.

### Disruption 1 · Subscription collapsed the price of a studio

A plugin collection that cost thousands of dollars to assemble under perpetual licenses is now
rented for the price of two streaming subscriptions. The shift is not partial — **Waves has
stopped selling individual plugins entirely** and moved to subscription-only.

| Offer | Monthly | Annual | Scope |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **LANDR Studio** | from $8.25 (≈€7.15) | — | Plugins plus mastering, samples and distribution; new releases included |
| **Waves Essential** | $14.99 (≈€12.99) | — | 110+ plugins |
| **Plugin Alliance CORE** | $14.99 (≈€12.99) | $149.99 (≈€129.95) | Brainworx and partner catalog |
| **Slate Complete Access** | $19.99 (≈€17.32) | $199.99 (≈€173.25) | 150+ plugins; bundled with SSL interfaces |
| **Waves Ultimate** | $24.99 (≈€21.65) | — | 220+ plugins |
| **Plugin Alliance PRO** | $29.99 (≈€25.98) | $299.99 (≈€259.91) | Full catalog |

- **The hardware consequence is direct.** Compressors, EQs, channel strips, tape emulations and
  reverbs once bought as outboard units are now line items in a $15–30 (≈€13–26) monthly
  bundle. Every hour of that workflow is an hour not spent buying, racking or replacing
  hardware.
- **The commercial-studio consequence is worse.** The historical reason to book a room was
  access to equipment no individual could afford. Subscription removed that reason for most of
  the mid-tier work that used to fill those rooms.
- **The industry ate its own margin.** Slate's Complete Access is bundled free for three months
  with Solid State Logic interfaces — a hardware maker (both owned by Audiotonix) using a
  plugin subscription as a hardware inducement, precisely inverting the model plugins were
  originally sold on.

**The irony worth naming.** Plugin Alliance's subscription tiers helped establish the price
point that made perpetual-license plugin businesses hard to sustain. Francisco Partners bought
Plugin Alliance in April 2022 at the top of the market it had helped reprice; four years later
the founder bought it back out of the insolvency estate.

### Disruption 2 · AI moved work from engineers into software

AI in music production is no longer prospective. In a 2026 study by Water & Music and Moises,
**78% of professional musicians reported using AI for music-related work in the previous twelve
months**, against 60% of hobbyists — professionals are leading adoption, not resisting it.
Vocal isolation led task adoption at **71%**. A separate 2026 Sonarworks survey of **1,100+
producers** found **60% using AI as an ideation tool** and **30% as a co-producer** whose
suggestions reach the finished track.

The tasks AI has absorbed are precisely the ones that used to be billed by the hour: stem
separation, noise repair, reference-matching, mastering, vocal tuning, smart EQ, and level
balancing.

- **Mastering is the clearest displacement.** LANDR, eMastered, BandLab Mastering and
  CloudBounce dominate the volume tier. LANDR's own positioning targets creators who release
  frequently and need masters *"without involving a mastering engineer."* Flagship major-label
  work still goes to humans; the indie and mid-tier work that sustained most mastering
  practices does not.
- **It takes share from software as well as services.** An AI assistant that sets a chain in
  seconds reduces the number of specialist plugins a user feels they need — which is why AI
  features are now a defensive requirement for every plugin vendor, not a differentiator.
- **And it takes share from hardware.** The pitch for an outboard channel strip was always
  expertise embodied in a circuit. The pitch for an AI assistant is expertise embodied in
  software, at zero marginal cost, inside the DAW the customer already owns.

**The strategic tell:** the industry's leading AI-audio asset — **iZotope**, maker of RX and
Ozone — was acquired in July 2026 by **Boris FX**, a visual-effects software company. No
music-hardware incumbent won it.

**Confidence note.** The adoption percentages above come from vendor- and media-published
surveys with self-selected samples (Sonarworks, n>1,100; Water & Music / Moises). They are
directionally reliable and consistent with each other, but they are not audited panel data. No
independent syndicated tracker of AI adoption in music production exists.

## Part VIII — Implications

*Framed for an acquisitive hardware-and-software manufacturer of the inMusic type.*

1. **What you bought is a user base, and that is the right thing to have bought.** After the
   three-way split, the acquired asset is Kontakt, Traktor, Maschine and 25 million direct
   relationships. Govern on **retention of that base through the first two product cycles**,
   not on cost synergy — the plugin and mastering brands that would have justified a portfolio
   thesis went to other buyers.
2. **Do not plan against a recovering hardware market.** 2025's only supplier-level revenue
   growth was tariff pass-through, and US exports ran 8.4% below their four-year average.
3. **Cut the tail. Own a narrow vertical rather than a wide net at many price points.** The case
   for a broad catalog was operating leverage: one distribution network, one dealer
   relationship, many SKUs. Tariffs destroyed that logic — a 145% duty is levied per unit, so
   breadth now multiplies exposure instead of amortizing it, and every marginal SKU carries
   inventory, tooling, certification and support cost against a category that is not growing. The
   categories with real decade growth are narrow and specific (US synth/DJ/electronic +36.5%,
   fretted +38%). **Depth in one of those beats presence in ten.** The long tail is not
   optionality; it is carrying cost.
4. **Scale has become a liability where it used to be the moat — expect conglomerates to break
   up, not bulk up.** The 2026 record already shows it. The Native Instruments estate did not
   sell as a portfolio; it cleared only once it was **split three ways** to three focused owners.
   iZotope went to a company that does one thing well in an adjacent medium. Plugin Alliance and
   Brainworx went back to their founder — a small, family-held owner operating alongside Manley
   and Apogee. Sonova is unwinding Sennheiser Consumer; Masimo unwound Sound United; Bose
   divested its professional division. **Every large diversified owner in this report that
   assembled breadth has since sold it, and the buyers who made money were the focused ones.**
5. **For anyone selling software into subscription, lean is the only viable structure.**
   Subscription converts a lumpy, high-margin license business into a thin recurring one, and a
   thin recurring business cannot carry conglomerate overhead or sponsor leverage — precisely the
   arithmetic that ended Soundwide. A founder-run operation with a small team can survive at
   $15–30 (≈€13–26) per user per month; a private-equity-owned roll-up servicing acquisition debt
   on the same revenue cannot. **In plugins the efficient operating size is small, and the July
   2026 founder buy-back is the market demonstrating it.**
6. **Price the subscription question before a competitor does.** At $15–30 (≈€13–26) a month
   for 100–220 plugins, willingness to pay for perpetual software has been reset permanently. A
   hardware business that bundles software has an advantage a pure software business does not —
   SSL/Slate is already running that play.
7. **Treat AI as table stakes in software and as a threat in hardware.** 78% professional
   adoption means an AI-assisted workflow is the default context your hardware is evaluated in.
8. **Serato's independence is a regulatory artifact, not a durable market condition.**
9. **DJ and electronic is the growth exception — and the leader holds ~70%.** +36.5% US decade
   growth in a flat market makes this the most attractive and most concentrated category.
10. **Rebuild deal capability around distress, not auction** — standing committed capital, a
   pre-cleared integration playbook, and legal capacity in German and UK insolvency. 2026
   demonstrated that speed, not price, wins these processes.
11. **The market-data seat is open.** Reverb's dataset sits under a competitor's owner; MI
   SalesTrak is a small independent panel; NAMM and Music Trades publish annually without
   published methodology; no one measures AI adoption or subscription substitution at all.

## Methodology and limitations

**Sources.** Company disclosures (Etsy 10-Q, Masimo IR, Sonova, Roland IR, Spotify Q2 2026),
trade-association reporting (IFPI Global Music Report 2026, RIAA 2025 year-end, NAMM 2025
Global Report and tariff guidance), regulator publications (NZ Commerce Commission case
register), the Peterson Institute for International Economics on tariff effects, and industry
trade press.

**Confidence levels.**

- **Company-confirmed:** Etsy/Reverb ($105m, closed 2 June 2025, $5.1m loss on sale);
  Masimo/Harman ($350m, closed 23 September 2025); Sonova/Sennheiser (€200m); Roland/DW ($65m);
  STG/Avid ($1.4bn, $27.05/share); Spotify Q2 2026; IFPI and RIAA 2025; the NI insolvency date;
  the Boris FX/iZotope and RCKFRC/Plugin Alliance announcements.
- **Press-reported, not company-confirmed:** KKR/Pioneer DJ (~$551m); Noritsu/AlphaTheta
  (~$606m); AlphaTheta's ~70% DJ share; the ~100 NI redundancies and UK office closure; the
  characterization of NI's losses under Francisco Partners; the 2020–21 guitar-sales
  percentages and the 16m first-time-buyer estimate.
- **Survey data, self-selected samples:** all AI-adoption percentages (Sonarworks 2026,
  n>1,100; Water & Music / Moises 2026) and the "80% of manufacturers will raise prices" tariff
  survey.
- **Undisclosed:** transaction values for inMusic/Native Instruments, Boris FX/iZotope,
  RCKFRC/Plugin Alliance, inMusic/Moog, Fender/PreSonus, and all Focusrite and Audiotonix
  transactions. Where a value is absent above it was not disclosed — not omitted.

**Known gaps.**

1. **No transaction multiples.** Almost no deal in this sector discloses revenue or EBITDA
   multiples. Every valuation inference is drawn from entry-versus-exit price on the three
   outsider-buyer cases — the only place the arithmetic is public.
2. **US market sizing depends on two paywalled annual sources** (Music Trades Census, NAMM
   Global Report), neither of which publishes methodology or error bounds.
3. **No DJ-category revenue series exists.** DJ equipment has no dedicated commodity code and
   no syndicated tracker.
4. **The tariff-to-software transmission is inferred, not measured.** No public dataset isolates
   US plugin or DAW spending, so the claim that tariff-compressed discretionary budgets
   contributed to software distress is reasoned from adjacency, not demonstrated.
5. **Subscription substitution is unquantified.** No source measures how much hardware
   purchasing subscription bundles displace. The direction is clear; the magnitude is not.
6. **Private-company financials are unavailable** for inMusic, AlphaTheta, Audiotonix,
   Sweetwater, and Guitar Center beyond disclosed debt events.
7. **Announced transactions only.** Processes that failed before announcement, and minority
   investments below disclosure thresholds, are invisible here.
